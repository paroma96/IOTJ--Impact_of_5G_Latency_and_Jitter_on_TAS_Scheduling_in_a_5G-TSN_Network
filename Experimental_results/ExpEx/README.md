# Experiment Extra: Wired TSN network vs Wireless 5G-TSN network

<img width="787" height="801" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/d53a5847-18b5-4963-b18f-c20e1388e233" />

We analyze in a more precise way the port-to-port delay distribution for delay-critical (DC) traffic to characterize the latency profile and jitter introduced by the integration of a 5G system compared to a standard wired TSN bridge. In this setup, the network cycle is fixed at 30 ms and the transmission window ($W_{\text{DC}}$) is set to 46.5 μs, corresponding to the minimum window required for the traffic generation rate. The experiment focuses on Downlink (DL) traffic using a Time-Aware Shaper (TAS) mechanism to evaluate the determinism of the communication path.

The results highlight a significant divergence in performance between the two media. The Wired TSN Bridge exhibits a highly deterministic but multimodal distribution within a narrow range, with a minimum delay of 10.99 μs and a 99.9th percentile of 40.28 μs. The maximum delay value reaches 40.53 μs. Then, the jitter value defined in our article is 29.29 μs. This behavior confirms that wired TSN maintains microsecond-level precision, though it shows small fluctuations attributed to internal switching fabric contention and TAS synchronization.

In contrast, the 5G Wireless System introduces a much broader delay profile and a higher magnitude of jitter. The empirical delay for the 5G segment shows a minimum of 4.5 ms and a mean of 7.54 ms. Crucially, the 99.9th percentile reaches 14.86 ms, with maximum peaks extending up to 18.41 ms. This results in a 10.36 ms jitter. These millisecond-level variations represent the inherent unpredictability of the wireless channel, including scheduling latencies and retransmission mechanisms.

The experiment demonstrates that for a 5G system to successfully integrate into a TSN architecture, the downstream TAS windows must be dimensioned using the 99.9th percentile of the 5G delay (14.86 ms) as an uncertainty interval buffer. Failure to account for this jitter would result in packets arriving at the subsequent TSN switch outside their transmission windows, allocated in network cycles, causing them to be buffered for an entire additional network cycle (30 ms), thereby destroying end-to-end determinism. As observed, the 5G delay is several orders of magnitude higher than the wired equivalent, necessitating adaptive scheduling intervals based on the specific empirical conditions of the 5G deployment.
